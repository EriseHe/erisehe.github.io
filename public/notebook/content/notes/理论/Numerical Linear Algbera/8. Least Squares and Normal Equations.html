8. Least Squares and Normal Equations

2026-09-30

Numerical Methods I — Florian Schaefer. Source: Lecture 08.pdf, pp. 1–9. Schedule association: September 30, 2026, from the Brightspace syllabus.

8.1 Recap: Orthogonal Transformations and QR

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 1.

The preceding lecture discussed stored Householder reflectors, Givens rotations, full and reduced QR factorizations, and the existence and uniqueness of QR.

For a Householder vector \(v_k\), the corresponding reflector acts on an active vector by

\[ b_{k:m}\leftarrow b_{k:m}-2v_k\frac{v_k^*b_{k:m}}{v_k^*v_k}. \]

With the factor convention in the lecture, applying these updates for \(k=1,\ldots,n\) computes \(Q^*b\); reversing their order applies \(Q\). The factors can be used without explicitly constructing a dense orthogonal or unitary matrix.

A real Givens rotation eliminates one entry using

\[ c=\frac{A_{ii}}{\sqrt{A_{ii}^2+A_{ji}^2}},\qquad s=\frac{A_{ji}}{\sqrt{A_{ii}^2+A_{ji}^2}}, \]

when the denominator is nonzero. These transformations lead to a triangular factor while preserving Euclidean lengths.

8.2 Solving Overdetermined Least Squares

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 2.

Let \(A\in\mathbb K^{m\times n}\), \(m\ge n\), and \(b\in\mathbb K^m\), where \(\mathbb K=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\). We want to solve

\[ \min_{x\in\mathbb K^n}\|Ax-b\|_2^2. \]

If \(A\) has full column rank and \(A=QR\) is its reduced QR factorization, then \(Q^*Q=I_n\) and \(R\) is nonsingular and upper triangular. The expected answer is

\[ Rx=Q^*b, \qquad x=R^{-1}Q^*b. \]

The inverse is a mathematical expression; an implementation computes \(Q^*b\) and solves the triangular system. The geometric picture is that \(QQ^*b\) is the projection of \(b\) onto the column space of \(A\). We now justify this answer through an optimality condition.

8.3 Orthogonality of the Residual

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 3.

Write \(r=Ax-b\). For any perturbation \(h\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \|A(x+h)-b\|_2^2-\|Ax-b\|_2^2 &=\|Ah+r\|_2^2-\|r\|_2^2\\ &=\|Ah\|_2^2+\langle Ah,r\rangle+\langle r,Ah\rangle\\ &=\|Ah\|_2^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle Ah,r\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\langle u,v\rangle=u^*v\). The signs in this expansion correct an inconsistency in the handwritten intermediate line.1

If \(x\) is a minimizer, set \(h=tz\) for arbitrary \(z\) and real \(t\). The expression

\[ t^2\|Az\|_2^2+2t\operatorname{Re}\langle Az,r\rangle \]

must be nonnegative for positive and negative \(t\). Its linear coefficient is therefore zero. Over \(\mathbb C\), applying the same argument to \(iz\) also forces the imaginary part to vanish. Thus

\[ \boxed{\langle Az,Ax-b\rangle=0\quad\text{for every }z\in\mathbb K^n.} \]

The residual is orthogonal to every vector in \(\operatorname{range}(A)\).

Conversely, this condition makes the difference in squared residuals equal to \(\|Ah\|_2^2\ge0\), proving sufficiency as well.2 A minimizer is unique when \(A\) has full column rank: then \(Ah=0\) only for \(h=0\).

8.4 Why the QR Answer Works

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 4.

For square nonsingular \(A\), choose \(z=A^{-1}(Ax-b)\). Orthogonality yields

\[ 0=\langle Az,Ax-b\rangle=\|Ax-b\|_2^2, \]

so the least-squares solution solves \(Ax=b\) exactly. For tall or singular \(A\), not every vector in the ambient space is of the form \(Az\), and a nonzero residual may remain.

For full-column-rank \(A=QR\) and \(x=R^{-1}Q^*b\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle Az,Ax-b\rangle &=\langle QRz,QQ^*b-b\rangle\\ &=\langle Rz,Q^*QQ^*b-Q^*b\rangle\\ &=\langle Rz,Q^*b-Q^*b\rangle=0. \end{aligned} \]

Hence the QR construction satisfies the optimality condition and gives the unique least-squares minimizer.

8.5 The Normal Equations

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 5.

The residual condition can be expressed without QR:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle Az,Ax-b\rangle=0\quad\forall z &\iff\langle z,A^*(Ax-b)\rangle=0\quad\forall z\\ &\iff A^*(Ax-b)=0. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{A^*Ax=A^*b.} \]

This is a square system even when \(A\) is rectangular. Its coefficient matrix is symmetric over \(\mathbb R\) and Hermitian over \(\mathbb C\).

The drawback is conditioning. For full-column-rank \(A\),

\[ \kappa_2(A^*A)=\kappa_2(A)^2. \]

This is the precise 2-norm form of the sheet’s “squared condition number” warning: forming and solving the normal equations can lose accuracy relative to working directly with QR.

8.6 Hermitian and Positive Semidefinite Matrices

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 6.

A square matrix is Hermitian if \(A^*=A\), and skew-Hermitian if \(A^*=-A\). For real matrices these become symmetric and skew-symmetric. Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues; skew-Hermitian matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues, including possibly zero.

A Hermitian matrix is positive semidefinite if

\[ v^*Av\ge0\quad\text{for every }v, \]

and positive definite if the inequality is strict for every \(v\ne0\). The sheet uses “positive” for the non-strict inequality; we distinguish these two cases explicitly.

The page also mentions the polar decomposition of a square complex matrix:

\[ A=US, \]

where \(U\) is unitary and \(S\) is Hermitian positive semidefinite. This separates a unitary transformation from a positive stretching factor. No uniqueness claim is needed here.

8.7 Gram Matrices

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, pp. 7–8; these pages substantially duplicate one another.

Let \(B\in\mathbb K^{r\times n}\). The entries of \(B^*B\) are

\[ \begin{aligned} (B^*B)_{ij} &=\langle e_i,B^*Be_j\rangle\\ &=\langle Be_i,Be_j\rangle\\ &=\langle B_{:,i},B_{:,j}\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(B^*B\) is the Gram matrix, or inner-product matrix, of the columns of \(B\).

TheoremGram matrices and positivity

A square matrix is Hermitian positive semidefinite if and only if it can be written as \(B^*B\). Moreover, \(B^*B\) is positive definite if and only if \(B\) has full column rank.

8.7.1 Every Gram Matrix Is Positive Semidefinite

We have

\[ (B^*B)^*=B^*B, \qquad x^*B^*Bx=\|Bx\|_2^2\ge0. \]

The squared norm is essential; the handwritten line omits its exponent.3

8.7.2 Every Positive Semidefinite Matrix Is a Gram Matrix

For Hermitian positive semidefinite \(A\), write a spectral decomposition

\[ A=U\Lambda U^*,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),\quad\lambda_i\ge0. \]

Set

\[ B=\Lambda^{1/2}U^*. \]

Then \(B^*B=U\Lambda U^*=A\). The source uses the equivalent convention \(A=U^*\Lambda U\); the convention above is used consistently throughout this proof.

8.7.3 Positive Definiteness and Full Column Rank

Source: Lecture 08.pdf, p. 9.

\[ \begin{aligned} B\text{ is column-rank deficient} &\iff\exists x\ne0:Bx=0\\ &\iff\exists x\ne0:\|Bx\|_2^2=0\\ &\iff\exists x\ne0:x^*B^*Bx=0. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, the normal-equations matrix \(A^*A\) is positive definite exactly when the least-squares matrix \(A\) has full column rank. This prepares the way for Cholesky factorization.
